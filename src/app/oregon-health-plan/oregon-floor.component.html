Floor and top-up: an idea I ran the numbers on, and why I would not do it

Warning: I do not recommend this. It is not the board's plan and it is not part of my own proposal. A friend suggested a smaller public plan at a lower tax, with private insurance for the rest. I worked out what it would do, and I do not like the answer. This page shows the work.

Warning: the numbers are rough. Every figure marked my math is my own estimate from the board's documents, resting on assumptions I name. No actuary has priced this.

The idea

The board's draft covers nearly everything, with few or no copays. This idea covers less in the public plan, at a lower tax, and lets people and employers buy the rest.

The idea · public, everyone

The floor

One basic public plan for every Oregonian, paid for by the same taxes as the draft, at lower rates.

Covers
Doctor and hospital care, mental health and addiction care, prescriptions, and children's dental and vision
No copays
For checkups and screenings, and for everyone below the income where the new household tax starts (the exemption)
Above that
Copays, up to a yearly limit: a share of the household's income above the exemption
Medicaid
No change. People on Medicaid (the Oregon Health Plan) keep everything they have now
The idea · private, optional

The top-up

Add-on insurance, bought by a household or by an employer as a benefit. It adds to the floor and never replaces it.

Covers
Adult dental, vision, hearing and fertility care and other extras, and perhaps the floor's copays (an open question, below)
Who buys it
Anyone who wants it. Nobody has to
What it cannot do
Stand in for the floor, or move anyone ahead in line for care the floor covers
Cost
Unknown. Nothing in the documents says what one would cost

Three questions the draft leaves open

Copays

Not decided

The board prefers none and says targeted copays bring "affordability and administrative burden considerations", but it "has not established a final cost-sharing policy" (legislative brief, pages 2 and 3). In plain terms: copays can keep people from care, and billing patients costs money

Private insurance alongside the plan

Not discussed

Whether insurers "would primarily serve a supplemental role", selling extra coverage on top: "This topic was not discussed by the Board" (brief, page 12)

Which plan pays the bill first when a worker has both

Put off

"The Board discussed this issue but deferred a policy decision" (brief, page 12)

A floor and a top-up is one answer to all three: some copays, insurers selling extras, and the public plan paying first. It is not the answer I would pick.

Why I looked at it

Vermont, 2014

11.5% + 9.5%

The payroll tax, and an income tax of up to 9.5%, that its plan needed. The governor who had championed it withdrew it

Colorado, 2016

79% no

Voters on a universal plan paid for by a 10% tax on payroll and other income

Oregon's draft, Model 3: employers + households

9.6–14% + 7.7%

Employer payroll tax and household tax rate at the board's cost estimate. About 30% higher at the estimate of Milliman, its actuary (cost expert)

Medicare, since 1965

A floor and a top-up

Medicare without an extra plan leaves 20% of most doctor bills and has no yearly limit. Nearly nine in ten people on it fill the gaps with other coverage, most often a Medigap policy or a former employer's plan. About half of everyone on Medicare now picks a private plan in its place, which this idea would not allow

No state has run a single public plan for everyone. Vermont's governor withdrew his plan the day he presented its rates, and Colorado's voters rejected theirs. Both failed largely on the size of the tax. Medicare has always covered less and let people add to it, and its gaps are a much-criticized feature. That was my reason to price a thinner plan. It is not proof one would pass, and the section at the top is why I stopped there.

Where the numbers come from

  • Dental, vision and more. The draft plan spends $4.5 to $5.1 billion on dental, vision, fertility, hearing aids and a few smaller benefits (report, Table E6). Medicaid's part stays in the floor. I put it between its $0.8 billion of dental funding (report, page 28) and a quarter of the total, its share of Oregonians. I assume {{ pct(children.low) }} to {{ pct(children.high) }} of the rest is for children and stays too.
  • Copays. Oregonians paid about $3.5 billion out of their own pockets in 2022, leaving out long-term care and drugstore items (Appendix FR, Figure AFR1a, an estimate from national figures). At 5.3% growth a year, the lower of the brief's two rates, that is ${{ oop }} billion in 2032. It includes people on Medicare, today's dental bills and some care the plan would not cover, so I assume the floor's copays keep only {{ pct(kept.low) }} to {{ pct(kept.high) }} of it. That share stands in for a copay schedule. It is the number an actuary would replace.
  • Billing. The draft counts two provider savings (report, page 23 and Table E7). One is $3.0 to $3.3 billion from providers collecting every dollar and "no longer needing to bill individuals". I assume that is lost in step with the copays. The other is $4 to $5 billion in billing and eligibility work. A clinic that bills any patient keeps its billing office, so I assume {{ pct(adminLost.low) }} to {{ pct(adminLost.high) }} of that is lost too.
  • Not counted. Two things that would make the floor look worse: a top-up that pays the copays brings back care the copays discouraged, and the public plan pays for it; and insurers selling a top-up have their own overhead. One that would make it look better: people use less care when it is not free. Some of that is care they did not need, and some is care they did.

Who pays less, and who pays more

Pays less than under the draft
  • Everyone who pays the new taxes, by roughly {{ pctSpan(cut.actuary) }} of those taxes ({{ pctSpan(cut.board) }} at the board's estimate), before any copays or top-up.
  • People who use little care and skip the top-up. They keep the whole tax cut.
  • Healthy higher earners. The tax cut grows with income. A healthy household gains more the more it earns. A sick one above the exemption will usually pay more.
  • Employers that offer no top-up. One that offers it pays for it on top of the payroll tax.
Pays more than under the draft
  • People who are sick, above the exemption. A household with a chronic illness reaches the yearly limit every year.
  • People who put off care because of a copay, and pay for it later in worse health.
  • Middle-income households that use a lot of care. A limit tied to income softens this and does not remove it.
  • Adults who need dental work, glasses, hearing aids or fertility treatment and have no top-up.
  • Clinics and hospitals, which go on billing patients, bill a second insurer for anyone with a top-up, and write off the copays they cannot collect.
  • Oregon as a whole. By my math about {{ billions(actuary.givenBack) }} more is spent in total, before the costs listed under "Not counted" and any drop in use.

Compared with today the picture is different. Most households above the exemption already pay premiums, deductibles and copays, and under a floor the premium is gone. I cannot show household bills the way my opinion page does: that takes a copay schedule and a top-up price, and neither exists yet.

What it cannot do

Limits
  • It cannot be thin for everyone. People who qualify for Medicaid "would keep access to every service Medicaid requires" (report, page 9). That is about one Oregonian in four in the report's 2032 figures.
  • It gives up part of the simpler system. The draft's savings come from one payer and no patient billing. A floor keeps some of both, and the plan, not the front desk, would have to say at every visit what each patient owes.
  • It makes two tiers. A rule that a top-up cannot buy a place in line would have to be written into law, and it does not stop a clinic from preferring patients whose copays an insurer pays. Some coverage would again depend on your job.
  • If the public plan pays first, as here, it may be harder to defend in court. The brief says paying first "could also be vulnerable" to challenge under the federal law that governs employer plans (page 12). That is true of the draft too.
  • It does not fix the cost estimate. The tax rates would still assume the lower of two cost estimates unless rates are printed for both.
What I have not worked out
  • The copays and the yearly limit. Too low and the tax barely moves; too high and people skip care they need.
  • Whether a top-up should be allowed to pay the copays. If it does, the copays stop discouraging anything and the public plan pays for the extra care. If it does not, there is less reason to buy one.
  • What a top-up costs, and whether insurers would sell one at a price people would pay.
  • Medicare. People on it already have a floor with gaps. Whether Oregon's floor fills those gaps is a separate decision, and my copay figure does not separate them out.
  • How it fits my own proposal. I have not tried to combine it with the proposal on my opinion page.

Where I come down: I would not do this. If the board wants to settle the question, its actuary could price the plan at two benefit levels, the draft's and a floor with copays tied to income, and show what a household would pay under each. My own proposal is on my opinion page.
