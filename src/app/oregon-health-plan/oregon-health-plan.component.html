What Oregon's Universal Health Plan would actually do

A state board has spent three years designing one public health plan for everyone who lives in Oregon, paid for with taxes instead of premiums. Its report runs 77 pages. This page is the short version: what you would get, what you would pay, and what nobody has decided yet.

It is a proposal, not law. Nothing changes unless the Legislature acts, starting in 2027.

The short version

Who is covered

Everyone who lives in Oregon

Once the plan is fully phased in. Coverage would not depend on your job, age or income. Medicare, VA and similar federal coverage would continue alongside it.

What you pay for care

No premiums

You would pay a tax based on income instead. The board's goal is no copays or deductibles either, but that part is not final.

What pays for it

New taxes

A household contribution on income above a set level, a tax on employers' payroll (the wages they pay) above $500,000, and smaller business taxes.

How much

Not decided

The board sent five tax models and did not recommend one. A middle-income household would pay under 1% to 2.5% of its income, or 8% to 10% counting the payroll tax on its wages.

Who comes out ahead

Depends on your situation

The board says most people would pay the same or less. In its examples, higher incomes pay more. Its appendix adds that in the models without a worker credit, most public employees and most single workers with job-based coverage would pay more too.

When

No date is set

A phased start could come one to two years after a law passes. Moving everyone at once, Medicare included, would more likely take ten years or more. The cost estimates use 2032.

Where it stands

  1. 2023 Senate Bill 1089 creates the Universal Health Plan Governance Board: nine volunteers appointed by the governor and confirmed by the Senate.
  2. Oct 2026 The board takes up its draft report to the Legislature. It lays out the plan's design and five ways to fund it.
  3. Dec 1, 2026 The final report is due to the Legislature.
  4. 2027 Lawmakers decide whether to act, whether to do it in one bill or two, and whether to send any of it to voters. The board is asking for funding to keep working on the design through 2029.
  5. After a law The board has not chosen how to start. Beginning with people the state can cover on its own, about half of Oregonians, could take one to two years. Adding the Oregon Health Plan and Marketplace would cover about 82% and could take two to five years. Medicare would come last and needs federal permission that does not exist today.
  6. 2032 The year the cost estimates use, chosen as the earliest the plan could be fully running. They assume it covers everyone by then, sooner than the report's own timeline for Medicare.

What the plan would cover

Benefits are modeled on the plan Oregon's public employees have today, with broader mental health coverage added.

  • Any qualified provider who takes the plan
  • No restrictive networks
  • No bills beyond what the plan pays

Whether doctors and hospitals would have to take the plan, or could choose, has not been decided.

Limits worth knowing

  • Long-term care Not included, unless you already qualify for it through Medicaid.
  • Your first six months New enrollees get primary, behavioral health, emergency and medically necessary care right away. The full package, including planned (elective) outpatient procedures and fertility services, starts after six months of continuous coverage. The report does not say where dental, vision or prescriptions fall.
  • Care outside Oregon Covered, but may come with some copays or deductibles, especially for non-emergency care.
  • Copays The board is still weighing limited copays for some high-cost drugs and elective services.

If this is your coverage today

Kept; most would owe nothing

Oregon Health Plan

You would keep your coverage and every benefit Medicaid requires. The board says most people who qualify by income would have no health care costs under the plan.

You would be covered

No insurance

You would get the full plan. You would pay the household contribution only if your income is above the exemption.

Your employer decides

Insurance through work

Federal law keeps the state from ending employer plans, so your employer could keep its plan or drop it. Whether the state plan would pay first or second when you have both is undecided. The payroll tax is meant to apply to every employer either way, and no offset for keeping a plan has been proposed.

Likely replaced

A plan you buy yourself

The state plan would cover what it does. Insurers could still sell supplemental coverage; the board has not discussed whether they could keep selling plans that duplicate the state's.

Stays

Medicare

Current law gives Oregon no way to fold Medicare into a state plan. The board recommends the plan pay your Part B premium (Part B is Medicare's doctor and outpatient coverage), pending more study. The report does not say what happens to Medigap or Part D.

Stays separate

VA, TRICARE, federal employee and Tribal health

Members of federally recognized Tribes could choose to join the state plan.

How you would sign up

  1. Say Oregon is your home and you intend to stay. Basic coverage would start right away, and you could list your dependents at the same time. Planned procedures and some other benefits wait six months, as noted above.
  2. Show proof within 60 days. Proof of where you live, such as a lease or a utility bill, plus your income and identity. The state would check its own records first.
  3. Stay covered automatically. Filing an Oregon tax return, or already being in a state program such as the Oregon Health Plan or SNAP, would renew you. The board expects that to cover 90% to 95% of people with no action on their part.

How it would be paid for

The idea is to replace what households and employers already spend on premiums, deductibles and copays with new taxes. The report assumes existing federal and state health funding continues, which depends on federal waivers, and covers more than half the cost. That leaves $30 billion to $39 billion a year for new state taxes to raise.

A little more than half

Employer payroll tax

Employers pay it; it is not withheld from paychecks. But like today's employer premiums, which the board's consultants call "typically embedded in compensation," much of it may come out of wages over time.

Only payroll above $500,000 a year is taxed, at 9.6%, so there is no cliff: a $520,000 payroll would owe 9.6% of $20,000, or $1,920. In four of the five models the rate rises to 14% on payroll above $10 million.

About 40%

Household contribution

Collected through your Oregon income tax return, on the income shown there (your adjusted gross income). Social Security does not count.

Income up to twice the federal poverty level, a yardstick the government sets by household size, is not taxed in three models: $31,920 for one person, $66,000 for a family of four. Only income above that is taxed.

About 5%

Business taxes

An extra 4.25 points on the corporate income tax, which is 6.6% and 7.6% today, and an extra 0.12 points on the Corporate Activity Tax, now 0.57%. One model adds a small statewide lodging tax to help cover care for visitors.

The five models

All five were set to raise the same money. They differ in who it comes from, and three choices drive the difference.

A credit for workers, or not

With the credit, half of the payroll tax your employer paid on your wages comes off your own bill. Fewer people pay, and those who do pay a higher rate. Self-employed people get no credit, so they pay more than most employees with the same income.

How much income is exempt

Either $66,000 or $45,540 for a family of four. In January 2026 the board voted 8 to 0 for the higher exemption. Models 4 and 5 use the lower one: more people pay, at lower rates.

One rate, or steps

One rate is simple to explain. Stepped rates, like income tax brackets, move more of the household contribution onto the highest earners.

How this is calculated, and what it leaves out
  • Each slice of your income above the exemption is charged at that slice's rate, the worker credit is subtracted, and the result is never less than zero. This matches the board's own example tables to within $4 for all five models.
  • The rates and brackets come from Appendix FR of the draft report. Its dollar brackets are in 2022 dollars; we bring them to 2026 by the growth in the one-person poverty level, which is what reproduces the board's examples.
  • The worker credit is half of the payroll tax your employer would pay on your wages. For a private employer we work that out from the payroll you enter. Public employers would pay the top rate from the first dollar.
  • If you have an employer, all your income is treated as wages. Investment, rental or business income would raise your bill, because the credit only applies to wages.
  • Amounts are in today's dollars and compared with what you pay today. The plan would not start for years, and today's premiums are likely to be higher by then.
  • Today's premiums are often paid before tax, and the self-employed can deduct them. The report does not say whether the household contribution would be deductible. If you deduct premiums today, your income would be higher without that deduction; that is our reading, not the report's.
  • Treat these as a low case. The rates are sized to the board's own cost estimate; at Milliman's higher estimate the taxes would have to raise about 30% more.
  • These rates were set on 2022 tax data. The Legislative Revenue Office's 2032 projection for Model 1 uses 12.0% instead of 13.5%, with the employer payroll tax raised to 9.89%, so expect all five to move.
  • Household size stands in for the number of exemptions on your tax return, as the appendix intends. Married couples filing separately have their own schedule, which is not modeled here.
  • Income here means federal adjusted gross income, less the Social Security counted in it. The appendix also leaves out interest on U.S. savings bonds and Treasury obligations and some older federal pensions.
  • A model's top rate applies only to the highest slice of income, not to all of it.
  • Poverty-level figures are for 2026. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

What the documents do and do not say

  • No cliff at $500,000 The rates are marginal by design, "to avoid fiscal cliffs." Crossing the line taxes only the payroll above it. The thresholds are meant to rise with inflation.
  • Your workers' credit depends on your size In the models with a worker credit, employees get back half of the tax you paid on their wages. A small firm pays little or none, so its employees get little or no credit.
  • Tax treatment The report says the payroll tax would be exempt from state and federal taxes the way employer health contributions are today. Federal treatment is not the state's to decide.
  • Wages The report expects that replacing premiums with a payroll tax "will likely keep wages at a lower level because nothing will force wages up when the premium cost goes away." Whether an employer that saves money shares it is left to the employer.
  • Not decided: what counts as payroll The report's examples count only wages. Nothing settles how owner draws, distributions or contractor payments are treated, and the appendix expects that without a worker credit both sides would have a reason to shift to contractor arrangements. The appendix intends the tax to be figured each pay period, not once a year, but does not say how the $500,000 is spread across pay periods; this calculator uses yearly totals. The board has not studied workers paid on a 1099.
  • Not decided: multi-state and remote workers The report calls the $500,000 a "firmwide payroll threshold." It does not say whether payroll outside Oregon counts, or how the tax treats Oregon residents working elsewhere and people who live in one state and work in another.
  • Not decided: which plan pays first If the state plan pays first and yours fills in around it, the report sees "possible disruption to benefits won through collective bargaining" and added legal risk under federal benefits law. If your plan pays first and the state plan wraps around it, less changes and less is saved.
  • Not decided: insured plans versus self-funded ones Federal law shields self-funded plans from state rules. Plans bought from an insurer are different: the state regulates insurers, and the brief says the future role of that coverage "was not discussed by the Board."
  • Not decided: when the tax starts The brief says lawmakers must decide when employer and household payments begin, how they apply to people not yet moved to the plan, and how to "minimize overlapping payment obligations" during a phased start. The thresholds, tiers and rates are themselves still open.

If you work in health care

The plan's cost savings depend on how providers are paid, so this is where much of the open work sits. One standard payment system would replace each insurer's own rates.

  • Primary care Mostly a set payment per patient paid in advance, plus fees for some services, monthly payments for running the practice, and payments tied to quality. At least 15% of the plan's spending would go to primary care. That is a share of spending, not a rate for any one practice.
  • Hospitals Mostly a fixed yearly budget covering about 70% of a hospital's payments, phased in over three to five years. It would also cover physician services at acute care hospitals. Critical access hospitals would get budgets too, which may be structured in line with Medicare's cost-based "reasonable cost" approach.
  • Specialists and everyone else For specialists paid outside a hospital budget, behavioral health, oral health, pharmacy and others: not chosen. The report says the board "did not select detailed payment approaches for specialty care sectors."
  • More, less or the same? Not determined. The report says it depends on "how the standardized fee schedule is structured and each provider's payer mix." It does not say what the standard rates would be tied to.
  • The savings assume lower rates About 10% below what the plan would otherwise pay, on average. our math The reasoning is that providers would collect every dollar billed and need fewer billing staff hours, leaving them, in the report's words, "paid approximately the same as they are now" overall. It does not say how that would fall on practices that rely on commercial rates, or what happens to billing staff.
  • Payment in full Providers who take the plan would accept its payment as payment in full and could not bill patients the difference. Whether taking the plan would be required has not been decided; the board has not discussed it.
  • More patients, same workforce More insured people, pent-up demand and little or no cost sharing would mean more demand: $5.7 to $8.7 billion a year of added care in the estimates. The report flags staffing shortages and thin margins as a risk but does not estimate whether there are enough clinicians.
  • Protections named, not designed The report mentions phasing in new rates, adjustments for "rural, safety net, or other vulnerable providers," and transition support for fragile ones. No rural or safety-net adjustment has been specified yet. The report expects that some providers and health systems "may resist" where standard rates or fixed budgets would cut their revenue.

What is not settled

The report is candid about how much is open. These are the ones most likely to change what you would pay or get.

  • The tax rates No model is recommended, and the board says the rates must be recalculated with current data before the plan is implemented. All five were set on 2022 tax data. Only Model 1 has a 2032 projection so far: a 12.0% household rate instead of 13.5%, with a higher payroll tax. That projection is sized to raise the board's $30 billion; at Milliman's $39 billion the taxes would have to raise about 30% more. The report says "all figures need outside vetting."
  • Your wages The report says the cost of health benefits "has held wages down over time," and that replacing an employer's insurance cost with a payroll tax "will likely keep wages at a lower level because nothing will force wages up when the premium cost goes away." It chose a payroll tax anyway to keep health money untaxed, and the worker credit is partly a response to this.
  • The wider economy The tax estimates assume nobody changes behavior, and the cost estimates assume a plan that is already fully running. A study of effects on wages, prices, jobs and migration has not been done.
  • Start-up money Start-up costs have not been estimated. The plan would also need about $21 billion in reserves, with no settled way to raise it.
  • Employer plans About two-thirds of workers with job-based coverage are in plans the state cannot regulate, by a national survey the brief cites. How many employers would switch is unknown, and the report says large, multi-state or self-funded employers "may have less reason or ability" to.
  • Provider pay and participation Whether doctors and hospitals would be paid more, less or the same is undetermined, and so is whether they must take the plan. The cost savings assume lower rates.
  • Federal approval Keeping Medicaid and Marketplace money requires federal waivers that can only be sought after the Legislature acts. Medicare cannot be folded in under current law.
  • Copays The board prefers none but has not set a final policy.
  • Timing and a public vote There is no launch date, and the board has not chosen how to phase people in. Moving everyone at once, including Medicare, would take six years at best and more likely "a minimum of ten years, if such pathways even become available." Lawmakers could also refer the plan to voters.

If nothing changes

The open questions above are the plan's. The report sets them beside the costs of staying put: "Every path forward carries risk, including making no changes at all."

What the board says the plan would fix

  • Coverage that stays put It would not end when a job, income or eligibility changes.
  • The same or less for most people With little or no cost sharing and no surprise bills. The report calls its figures group averages.

How covering everyone could cost about the same

Covering more people with broader benefits costs more. The plan gets back to the current system's total by spending less on administration and billing, and by paying providers lower rates in exchange for full, reliable payment.

The last two savings, $7.8 billion together, depend on the plan setting payment rates about {{ pct(rateReset.best, 0) }} below what it would otherwise pay providers. our math The reasoning: providers would collect every dollar billed and need less billing staff, so lower rates would leave them "paid approximately the same as they are now." The report adds: "If those renegotiations do not occur, these savings cannot be realized."

The size of the "dental, vision, hearing" and "administration" steps is our math: the plan's figure less the current system's. The report's current-system figure counts dental care but not what people spend today on vision, hearing aids or fertility care, so that step overstates the added cost. Steps may not add exactly because the report rounds each figure.

The published estimates, and what they depend on

The two published estimates put the plan about 1% to 9% below what the current system would cost in 2032. Both assume provider rates are reset. The last two rows are our own sensitivity check, not the board's: if rates were not reset, providers would in effect be paid about 10% more than today, and the plan would cost 2% to 9% more. our math

Milliman's rows are measured against a current system costing $78.3 billion. The board's rows assume slower cost growth for both the plan and the current system ($72.8 billion), so part of the gap between the two pairs is the growth rate, not the plan. The report calls the board's figure "roughly 8% lower"; $6.6 billion of $72.8 billion is 9%. The board's drug and fraud savings come from studies of national plans, and the report says a single state "would not be able to realize the savings of a national program." The board also lowered Milliman's administration estimate from 4.6% of spending to 3%. The report says members discussed that this "could be considered an ambitiously low estimate" and concluded it "is believed to be attainable."

What Oregon employers and households would pay

The new taxes are large because they replace spending of about the same size by employers, including public employers, and households. The totals match; who pays does not, and some households and employers would pay more than today. The report assumes federal and state health funding of $40 to $42 billion continues either way, which depends on federal waivers.

"Today" is our math: the report's current-system total less the public funding it expects to continue. The report's own summary adds the $3.5 billion of Part B premiums to the plan's side only, and shows the plan $2.9 billion above the current system. The board added them on purpose: the state would have to raise that money, and each $1 of Part B premium brings in about $3 of federal Medicare funding. It also says the topic "requires further evaluation." Medicare enrollees or their former employers pay those premiums today, so we count them on both sides, and the plan comes out about $0.6 billion lower on Milliman's estimate, the same gap the report shows before Part B. That is our reading; the report does not say whether its public-funding figure is already net of those premiums.

All figures come from Tables E2 to E11 of the draft report, for a plan fully running in 2032. They leave out start-up costs, about $21 billion in reserves, and long-term care.
