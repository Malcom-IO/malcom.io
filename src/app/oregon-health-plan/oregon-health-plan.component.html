What Oregon's Universal Health Plan would actually do

A state board has spent three years designing one public health plan for everyone who lives in Oregon, paid for with taxes instead of premiums. Its report runs 77 pages. This page is the short version: what you would get, what you would pay, and what nobody has decided yet.

It is a proposal, not law. Nothing changes unless the Legislature acts, starting in 2027.

Updated {{ updated }} · from the board's October 2026 draft report

The short version

Who is covered

Everyone in Oregon

Coverage would not depend on your job, age or income.

What you pay at the doctor

No premiums

The board's goal is no copays or deductibles either, but that part is not final.

What you pay instead

Two new taxes

One on household income above a set level, and a payroll tax paid by employers with more than $500,000 in payroll.

How much

Not decided

The board sent five tax models, with top household rates between 7.7% and 14.3%, and did not recommend one.

Who comes out ahead

Depends on your income

In the board's examples, lower and middle incomes mostly pay less than today and higher incomes pay more. The model and your employer matter a lot.

When

2032 at the earliest

No start date is set. That is the first year the board's actuaries think the plan could be fully running.

Where it stands

  1. 2023 Senate Bill 1089 creates the Universal Health Plan Governance Board: nine volunteers appointed by the governor and confirmed by the Senate.
  2. Oct 2026 The board takes up its draft report to the Legislature. It lays out the plan's design and five ways to fund it.
  3. Dec 1, 2026 The final report is due to the Legislature.
  4. 2027 Lawmakers decide whether to act, whether to do it in one bill or two, and whether to send any of it to voters. The board is asking for funding to keep working on the design through 2029.
  5. 2032 The earliest year the board's cost estimates assume full operation.

Benefits are modeled on the plan Oregon's public employees have today, with broader mental health coverage added.

Limits worth knowing

  • Long-term care Not included, unless you already qualify for it through Medicaid.
  • Your first six months Primary, behavioral health, emergency and medically necessary care start right away. Elective outpatient procedures and fertility services start after six months.
  • Care outside Oregon Covered, but may come with some cost sharing.
  • Copays The board is still weighing limited copays for some high-cost drugs and elective services.

If you already have coverage

Your employer decides

Insurance through work

Federal law keeps the state from ending employer plans, so your employer could keep its plan or drop it. Whether the state plan would pay first or second when you have both is undecided.

Stays

Medicare

Current law gives Oregon no way to fold Medicare into a state plan. The board recommends the plan pay your Part B premium, pending more study.

Kept in full

Oregon Health Plan

You would keep every benefit Medicaid requires, including long-term care.

Stays separate

VA, TRICARE, federal employee and Tribal health

Members of federally recognized Tribes could choose to join the state plan.

How it would be paid for

The idea is to replace what households and employers already spend on premiums, deductibles and copays with two taxes. Existing federal and state health funding would keep flowing and cover more than half the cost. That leaves $30 billion to $39 billion a year for new state taxes to raise.

A little more than half

Employer payroll tax

Paid by employers, not deducted from paychecks. The first $500,000 of a firm's yearly payroll is exempt. Above that the rate is 9.6%, and in four of the five models it rises to 14% on payroll above $10 million.

About 40%

Household contribution

Collected through your Oregon income tax return. Income up to twice the federal poverty level is exempt in three models ($31,920 for one person, $66,000 for a family of four). Income above that is taxed at the model's rate.

About 5%

Business taxes

Add-ons to the corporate income tax (4.25%) and the Corporate Activity Tax (0.12%). One model adds a small statewide lodging tax to help cover care for visitors.

The five models

All five raise roughly the same money. They differ in who it comes from, and three choices drive the difference.

A credit for workers, or not

With the credit, half of the payroll tax your employer paid on your wages comes off your own bill. Fewer people pay, and those who do pay a higher rate.

How much income is exempt

Either $66,000 or $45,540 for a family of four. The higher exemption spares more lower earners and pushes the rate up for everyone else.

One rate, or steps

One rate is simple to explain. Stepped rates, like income tax brackets, move more of the cost onto the highest earners.

How this is calculated, and what it leaves out
  • Each slice of your income above the exemption is charged at that slice's rate, the worker credit is subtracted, and the result is never less than zero. This matches the board's own example tables to within $4 for all five models.
  • The rates and brackets come from Appendix FR of the draft report. Its dollar brackets are in 2022 dollars; we bring them to 2026 by the growth in the one-person poverty level, which is what reproduces the board's examples.
  • If you have an employer, all your income is treated as wages. Investment, rental or business income would raise your bill, because the credit only applies to wages.
  • These rates were set on 2022 tax data. The Legislative Revenue Office's 2032 projection for Model 1 uses 12.0% instead of 13.5%, with the employer payroll tax raised to 9.89%, so expect all five to move.
  • Household size stands in for the number of exemptions on your tax return, as the appendix intends. Married couples filing separately have their own schedule, which is not modeled here.
  • Poverty-level figures are for 2026. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

What is not settled

The report is candid about how much is open. These are the ones most likely to change what you would pay or get.

  • The tax rates No model is recommended, and the board says the rates must be recalculated with current data before the plan is implemented. All five were set on 2022 tax data. Only Model 1 has a 2032 projection so far: a 12.0% household rate instead of 13.5%, with a higher payroll tax.
  • Your wages The report's words: replacing an employer's insurance cost with a payroll tax "will likely keep wages at a lower level because nothing will force wages up when the premium cost goes away."
  • The wider economy The tax estimates assume nobody changes behavior. A study of effects on wages, prices, jobs and migration has not been done.
  • Start-up money Start-up costs have not been estimated. The plan would also need about $21 billion in reserves, with no settled way to raise it.
  • Employer plans About two-thirds of workers with job-based coverage are in plans the state cannot regulate. How many employers would switch is unknown.
  • Federal approval Keeping Medicaid and Marketplace money requires federal waivers. Medicare cannot be folded in under current law.
  • Copays The board prefers none but has not set a final policy.
  • Timing and a public vote There is no launch date, the plan may phase in by population, and lawmakers could refer it to voters.

How covering everyone could cost about the same

Covering more people with broader benefits costs more. The plan gets back to the current system's total by spending less on administration and billing, and by paying providers lower rates in exchange for full, reliable payment.

The last two savings, $7.8 billion together, depend on the plan setting payment rates about {{ pct(rateReset.best, 0) }} below what it would otherwise pay providers. our math The reasoning: providers would collect every dollar billed and need less billing staff, so lower rates would leave them "paid approximately the same as they are now." The report adds: "If those renegotiations do not occur, these savings cannot be realized."

The report's current-system figure counts dental care but not what people spend today on vision, hearing aids or fertility care, so the third row overstates the added cost. Steps may not add exactly because the report rounds each figure.

It hinges on provider rates and cost growth

Depending on the assumptions, the plan ranges from about 9% more than the current system would cost in 2032 to about 9% less.

Milliman's rows are measured against a current system costing $78.3 billion. The board's rows assume slower cost growth for both the plan and the current system ($72.8 billion), so part of the gap between the two pairs is the growth rate, not the plan. The report rounds the board's figure to "roughly 8% lower." It says board members discussed that administration at 3% of spending "could be considered an ambitiously low estimate" and concluded it "is believed to be attainable."

What Oregon employers and households would pay

The new taxes are large because they replace spending of about the same size by employers, including public employers, and households. The report assumes federal and state health funding of $40 to $42 billion continues either way, which depends on federal waivers.

"Today" is our math: the report's current-system total less the public funding it expects to continue. The report's own summary adds the $3.5 billion of Part B premiums to the plan's side only, because the state would have to raise that money, and shows the plan $2.9 billion above the current system. Medicare enrollees or their former employers pay those premiums today. Counted on both sides, the plan comes out about $0.6 billion lower on Milliman's estimate, the same gap the report shows before Part B.

All figures come from Tables E2 to E11 of the draft report, for a plan fully running in 2032. They leave out start-up costs, about $21 billion in reserves, and long-term care.
