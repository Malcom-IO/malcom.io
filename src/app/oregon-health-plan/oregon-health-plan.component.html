What Oregon's Universal Health Plan would actually do

A state board has spent three years designing one public health plan for everyone who lives in Oregon, paid for with taxes instead of premiums. Its report runs 77 pages. This page is the short version: what you would get, what you would pay, and what nobody has decided yet.

It is a proposal, not law. Nothing changes unless the Legislature acts, starting in 2027.

Updated {{ updated }} · from the board's October 2026 draft report

The short version

Who is covered

Everyone who lives in Oregon. Coverage would not depend on your job, age or income.

What you pay at the doctor

No premiums. The board's goal is no copays or deductibles either, but that part is not final.

What you pay instead

A new tax on household income above a set level, plus a payroll tax paid by employers with more than $500,000 in payroll.

How much

Not decided. The board sent five tax models with household rates topping out between 7.7% and 12.05%, and did not recommend one.

Who comes out ahead

In the board's examples, lower and middle incomes mostly pay less than today and higher incomes pay more. It depends heavily on the model and on where you work.

When

No start date is set. The board's actuaries treat 2032 as the earliest year the plan could be fully running.

Where it stands

  1. 2023 Senate Bill 1089 creates the Universal Health Plan Governance Board: nine volunteers appointed by the governor and confirmed by the Senate.
  2. Oct 2026 The board takes up its draft report to the Legislature. It lays out the plan's design and five ways to fund it.
  3. Dec 1, 2026 The final report is due to the Legislature.
  4. 2027 Lawmakers decide whether to act, whether to do it in one bill or two, and whether to send any of it to voters.
  5. 2032 The earliest year the board's cost estimates assume full operation.

What the plan would cover

Benefits are modeled on the plan Oregon's public employees have today, with broader mental health coverage added. You could see any qualified provider who takes the plan, with no restrictive networks, and providers could not bill you beyond what the plan pays.

Covered

  • Hospital, emergency and ambulance care
  • Primary and specialty care, preventive care and screenings
  • Prescription drugs, including specialty drugs
  • Mental health and substance use treatment, including crisis services
  • Dental and vision
  • Maternity, newborn, fertility and reproductive care
  • Lab work, imaging, rehabilitation and medical equipment
  • Hospice and palliative care

Limits worth knowing

  • Long-term care is not included, unless you already qualify for it through Medicaid.
  • New enrollees get primary, behavioral health, emergency and medically necessary care right away. Elective outpatient procedures and fertility services start after six months of coverage.
  • Care outside Oregon is covered, but may come with some cost sharing.
  • The board is still weighing limited copays for some high-cost drugs and elective services.

If you already have coverage

Insurance through work
Federal law keeps the state from ending employer plans, so your employer could keep its plan or drop it. Whether the state plan would pay first or second when you have both is undecided.
Medicare
Medicare stays. Current law gives Oregon no way to fold it into a state plan. The board recommends the plan pay your Part B premium, pending more study.
Oregon Health Plan
You would keep every benefit Medicaid requires, including long-term care.
VA, TRICARE, federal employee and Tribal health
These stay separate. Members of federally recognized Tribes could choose to join the state plan.

How it would be paid for

The idea is to replace what households and employers already spend on premiums, deductibles and copays with two taxes. Existing federal and state health funding would keep flowing and cover more than half the cost.

A little more than half

Employer payroll tax

Paid by employers, not deducted from paychecks. The first $500,000 of a firm's yearly payroll is exempt. Above that the rate is 9.6%, and in four of the five models it rises to 14% on payroll above $10 million.

About 40%

Household contribution

Collected through your Oregon income tax return. Income up to twice the federal poverty level is exempt in three models ($31,920 for one person, $66,000 for a family of four). Income above that is taxed at the model's rate.

About 5%

Business taxes

Add-ons to the corporate income tax (4.25%) and the Corporate Activity Tax (0.12%). One model adds a small statewide lodging tax to help cover care for visitors.

The five models

All five raise roughly the same money. They differ in who it comes from. A credit for workers and a higher exemption both mean fewer people pay, and a higher rate for those who do.

How this is calculated, and what it leaves out

  • Your bill is the model's rate times your income above the exemption, minus any credit, and never less than zero. For Models 2 and 3 this matches the board's self-employed, public employer and Medicare examples to the dollar.
  • If you have an employer, all your income is treated as wages. In the board's private-employer example the wages are lower than the listed income, so its household figures run higher than this calculator's. Investment, rental or business income would raise your bill, because the credit only applies to wages.
  • Your pay is assumed to stay the same. The report says an employer that stops paying premiums will not necessarily raise wages.
  • Model 1 uses the 12.0% rate the report states. The report's own Model 1 examples, shown below, work out to 13.5%.
  • Models 4 and 5 have no dollar figure because the report does not publish their bracket schedules. The board's examples below show what they cost four sample households.
  • Poverty-level figures are for 2026. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

What is not settled

The report is candid about how much is open. These are the ones most likely to change what you would pay or get.

  • The tax rates. No model is recommended, and the board says the rates must be recalculated with current data before the plan is implemented. Model 1 was sized for 2032; Models 2 to 5 were sized on 2022 tax data against a smaller target.
  • Your wages. The report's words: replacing an employer's insurance cost with a payroll tax "will likely keep wages at a lower level because nothing will force wages up when the premium cost goes away."
  • The wider economy. The tax estimates assume nobody changes behavior. A study of effects on wages, prices, jobs and migration has not been done.
  • Start-up money. Start-up costs have not been estimated. The plan would also need about $21 billion in reserves, with no settled way to raise it.
  • Employer plans. About two-thirds of workers with job-based coverage are in plans the state cannot regulate. How many employers would switch is unknown.
  • Federal approval. Keeping Medicaid and Marketplace money requires federal waivers. Medicare cannot be folded in under current law.
  • Copays. The board prefers none but has not set a final policy.
  • Timing and a public vote. There is no launch date, the plan may phase in by population, and lawmakers could refer it to voters.

The statewide math

Whether the plan costs Oregon less than the current system depends on whose assumptions you use. The board's actuary, Milliman, puts the plan itself slightly lower ($77.7 billion against $78.3 billion) and slightly higher once it also pays Medicare Part B premiums. The board, assuming more savings on administration and drug prices, puts it lower either way.
