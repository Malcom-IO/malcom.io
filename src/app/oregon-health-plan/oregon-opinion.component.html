Opinion · Marcus Malcom

Two things I would change in Oregon's Universal Health Plan

This is one person's opinion. The explainer takes no position on the plan, and nothing here changes it. The proposals and every figure marked my math are mine, not the board's.

1 · How to pay for it Pick Model 3, with three changes The board drafted five ways to pay for the plan and recommended none. I would take the third, and fix what it gets wrong. 2 · How people sign up Ask for proof on day one The draft enrolls anyone on a signed statement, with proof due 60 days later. I would ask for one document at sign-up.

Beyond the revenue model

Signing up on your word

The draft covers anyone who says Oregon is their home and that they intend to stay. Every taxpayer will ask what stops someone from saying it, getting care and leaving. The draft's answer is thin.

Starting benefits in full, before any proof

60 days

Proof of residency is due after coverage starts, not before (report, page 7)

One proof the committee would accept

A statement plus any mail

"Attestation of residency plus any mail addressed to applicant at Oregon address" (enrollment proposal, page 3)

If the plan drops you because it could not verify you

Sign again

They "can re-enroll by attesting that they still meet the residency standard" (report, page 8)

If the statement was false, or proof never comes

No penalty, no bill

Coverage depends on proof, but neither document names a penalty or says who pays for care already given

The draft makes new members wait six months for full benefits, and that wait covers less than it sounds like. From day one a new member gets primary care, behavioral health, emergency care and "medically necessary services." The only things the report names as waiting are planned outpatient procedures and fertility services.

My proposal: proof on day one

The committee chose a statement because it is simplest and keeps new residents covered without a gap. It is simplest only on day one. After that the plan has to go back to each new member its records cannot confirm, track a 60-day deadline for each one, and end coverage for those who miss it, who can then sign again and start a new 60 days. That is a paperwork problem waiting to happen. Asking at sign-up means the plan chases fewer people, and nobody twice.

So: keep cover for care that cannot wait, ask for proof up front, and make the statement mean something.

Part 1 · mine

Proof first

The state checks its own records, as the draft plans, but at sign-up and not after. A match, or one document, finishes enrollment that day: an Oregon license, a lease, a utility bill, a pay stub showing an Oregon home address, enrollment papers from a Tribe with Oregon lands. With no lease in your name, a letter counts: from a shelter, caseworker or public agency, or from the person you are staying with, signed under penalty of law, at an address the state has on record for them. A statement plus a piece of mail does not.

Part 2 · mine

Care that cannot wait, never waits

With no proof yet, a new member still gets emergency, urgent, primary and behavioral health care, prescriptions a month at a time, and any treatment a clinician says cannot safely wait, for up to 60 days. So do their children. The plan pays the hospital or clinic for this care whatever happens later. Other care the draft allows in the first six months starts the day proof arrives.

Part 3 · mine

One residency

The statement is signed under penalty of law, like an Oregon tax return, and goes to the Department of Revenue and no other agency: you cannot be an Oregon resident for care and another state's resident for tax. It has to be true the day you sign; leaving later for a job or family is no offense. It asks nothing about immigration status. Out-of-state students, seasonal farm workers and people in custody, whom the draft covers as exceptions, show a school, employer or facility record instead, and their taxes do not change. Tribal members may join or not, as the draft says.

Part 4 · mine

Pay it back

If the statement was false, the plan bills the person or their insurer. The test needs no proof of intent: by your own statement you owed an Oregon resident return for that year, and after a written notice you still filed none. Filing late clears it. If proof simply never arrives, membership ends at day 60, apart from treatment under way that a clinician says cannot stop, and nobody is billed for those 60 days. After that an emergency room still treats you, as the law requires, and bills you as it would any visitor. Signing up again takes proof, not a second statement.

What it costs, and what it cannot do
  • People with the least paper wait longest. That includes long-time Oregonians who file no return and have no lease, not only newcomers. Until proof arrives they get less than the draft gives them, and after day 60 only treatment that cannot stop.
  • For 60 days the plan still pays for urgent and everyday care on a statement alone, and past day 60 for treatment that cannot stop. That is the price of nobody waiting.
  • A signed statement and a possible bill will scare off some people who qualify, and clinics will have to judge what can wait.
  • It does not stop a short stay with real paperwork. A lease proves an address, not an intent to stay. Someone who rents a room, gets treated and leaves pays Oregon tax and the household contribution for those months if they have income, and nothing if they do not. The report names the risk; Milliman's estimate counts some of it.
  • Most bills will not be collected from someone who has left Oregon. The bill is a deterrent, not revenue.
  • Medicaid's rules still govern anyone who qualifies for Medicaid, and many people with the least paper do. For them much of this may not apply.
What I looked at and set aside
  • A waiting period based on time lived in Oregon. The committee weighed a "tenure-based distinction" and notes that Medicaid rules "prohibit states from imposing any durational residency requirements." In 1974 the Supreme Court struck down a one-year county residency wait for poor people's non-emergency care at county expense (Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa County). Asking for proof that someone lives here is different from asking how long they have lived here.
  • A longer wait for full benefits. It would land on every new member, and the same case law is a risk to the six months the draft already has.

What I would ask for: have the board's counsel test these four parts, and the draft's own six-month wait, against federal law. Parts 3 and 4 would need to be written into statute. And have the plan publish each year how many people enroll within a year of arriving, what their care costs, and how many leave within a year. Nobody has to guess at the size of this once it is counted.

Four smaller fixes

{{ usd100(marriagePenalty) }}

End the marriage penalty

Two single people exempt $63,840 between them; a married couple, $43,280. That costs a couple this much a year. Set the couple's exemption at twice the single's. my math

{{ usd(splitSaving) }}

Count related firms as one

Each $500,000 exemption is worth this much a year, so splitting one firm into several pays. The draft has no rule against it. my math

14%

Treat public employers like private ones

School districts, cities and the state would pay the top payroll rate from the first dollar, because they spend more of payroll on coverage today. In the board's example that is still less than the premium they pay now, but it is one public budget taxing another.

55%

Write in a fallback

More than half the money is the payroll tax, which a federal benefits law could be used to challenge. The committee said it "may want automatic triggers" on other taxes if that happens. None is written.

I cannot price the first and third: the documents do not give the number of married returns above the exemption or the size of public payrolls.

What I looked at and set aside

A hard ceiling

A household at $1,000,000 and one at $2,000,000 would pay the same.

A lower rate on more income

Lowering the exemption to 138% of poverty only gets the rate to about 6.6% to 7.0%, and it charges households that pay nothing today. my math

Moving money from payroll to households

Households would see more of the cost, but payroll tax is paid before federal tax, and the report does not say a household contribution would be. It would likely send more of Oregon's money to Washington.

What I would ask for: have the board's actuaries price Model 3 with a worker credit at a few levels and a slower climb at a few starting points, at both cost estimates, and show who pays more and who pays less, before the Legislature picks a model. My figures for the slower climb are rough; theirs would not be.
