Two more models the board should price

This is one person's opinion. The explainer takes no position on the plan, and nothing here changes it. Models 6 and 7 and every figure marked my math are mine, not the board's.

All five draft models make the same choice: most of the money through employers, where households do not see it, and a quarter to two fifths of the rest from the top 1% of tax filers. I would like two other choices priced before the Legislature picks.

Model 6: who comes out behind, and ahead

Behind
  • High earners, and the plan's reliance on them. The top 1% would supply about {{ pct(top1OfAll().model6) }} of all the new revenue, up from {{ pct(top1OfAll().draft) }} under Model 3, before any soft cap. my math This does not fix the problem I started with; the soft cap only eases it.
  • Oregon, in federal taxes. This is a central reason the board gives for the payroll tax: employer health spending is untaxed, and routing contributions through payroll "keeps the tax advantage that wages do not get," instead of the money "leaving Oregon as increased federal taxes." Model 6 moves about {{ bn(moved().low) }} to {{ bn(moved().high) }} a year from untaxed payroll to household payments. my math I have not sized the federal tax on it.
  • Public employees. The single public employee's own bill goes from {{ usd(publicSingle().now) }} today to about {{ usd100(publicSingle().own) }}. Their employers' tax falls, but a raise is not guaranteed.
  • Anyone whose employer keeps the savings. Nothing requires a payroll tax cut to become pay, and a cut passed on as wages draws federal income and payroll taxes, so a dollar of cut is less than a dollar of take-home.
  • The self-employed, retirees with income, and workers at employers under $500,000 of payroll. They have no employer share to shrink, and their household rate still rises.
Ahead
  • Voters and lawmakers. The cost is on the tax return, where it can be seen and argued about.
  • Employers. About {{ pct(payrollCut()) }} less payroll tax: {{ big(employers()[0].model6) }} instead of {{ big(employers()[0].draft) }} on a $1.1 million payroll, {{ big(employers()[1].model6) }} instead of {{ big(employers()[1].draft) }} on $190 million.
  • Workers whose employers pass it on, at about the same total.
  • Households in the middle, in one sense: they would know what the plan costs them.

The shape, not the numbers
  • Model 7
  • Draft, Model 3

A sketch with no scale. Where the lines cross is exactly what needs pricing.

Behind
  • Households just above the lower exemption. A family of four between $45,540 and $66,000 pays nothing under Model 3 and would pay something here.
  • Lower and middle incomes generally, as a share of income. It is less progressive than Model 3, by design.
  • The board's own direction. It recommended the 200% exemption in January 2026 after public comment on exactly this.
Ahead
  • High earners, through a lower rate that still rises with income. No cap, so $2,000,000 still pays more than $1,000,000.
  • The plan's footing. Less of the money would rest on the top 1%, the opposite of Model 6.
  • Federal tax treatment. The payroll tax is untouched, so nothing moves out of untaxed payroll, again unlike Model 6.
  • Simplicity. One rate, one exemption, no cap to explain.

Why not something smaller

A hard ceiling

A household at $1,000,000 and one at $2,000,000 would pay the same.

A soft cap alone

Gives up revenue at the top and changes nothing for the middle. The board left it out as less progressive.

A minimum contribution

Charges households that pay nothing today, including many on the Oregon Health Plan.

What I would ask for: have the board's actuaries price both, Model 6 with and without a soft cap and Model 7 at a few exemption levels, and show who pays more and who pays less under each, before the Legislature picks one.
