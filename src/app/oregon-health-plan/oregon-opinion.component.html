Opinion · Marcus Malcom

Put a ceiling on what a household pays

This page is one person's opinion. The explainer it comes from takes no position on the plan, and nothing here changes that page. The numbers marked as mine are my arithmetic, not the board's.

I built the explainer to understand Oregon's proposed Universal Health Plan. One thing in it bothers me enough to say so separately: the household contribution has no upper limit. Everyone gets the same coverage, and the price of it keeps rising with income forever.

What the draft does

Under all five tax models a household pays a share of its income above an exempt amount. For most households that works out to less than they pay for health care today. At the top it does not come close. In my examples a family of four earning $220,000 would pay about $9,500 to $12,700 a year. The same family at $1,000,000 would pay $72,000 to $129,000, and at $2,000,000, $149,000 to $272,000, for the same coverage. Four times the plan's average cost per Oregonian is about {{ usd(familyCeiling) }} a year. my math

That is not a fee for health care. It works as an income tax earmarked for health care. People can reasonably want that. But it should be chosen on purpose and called what it is, because it carries a risk the board's own finance committee described: a small number of very high earners would supply a large share of the money, and they are the households most able to change how they are paid or where they live. The committee's report says a soft cap was weighed partly to avoid a schedule that "could be perceived as punitive relative to current health care payments."

The amount at stake is large. The top 1% of tax filers would pay {{ pct(stake.shareLow) }} to {{ pct(stake.shareHigh) }} of the household contribution depending on the model, which is roughly ${{ stake.low }} billion to ${{ stake.high }} billion a year. my math A plan that covers everyone should not depend that heavily on a small group of households staying put.

What I would keep

Almost all of it. Coverage for everyone. Contributions that rise with income. The exemption that leaves lower incomes paying nothing. The employer payroll tax. For about nine households in ten, I would change nothing about what the draft asks.

Three ways to fix the top

1. A ceiling at the cost of your own coverage (my pick)

No household pays more than the plan's average cost for a household its size: about {{ usd(costPerPerson) }} a person on the actuary's 2032 estimate, so {{ usd(familyCeiling) }} for a family of four. my math That is an average across all ages, a typical working-age family costs less, and more than half of it is already paid by federal and state money, so it is a generous ceiling, not a tight one. Below the ceiling nothing changes. At the ceiling a family of four is contributing about {{ ceilingTimes }} times what the average family that size would, and the bill stops there. my math It is easy to explain, and it answers the "punitive" worry directly: nobody is charged more than the average cost of what they are getting.

It reaches further down than it sounds. With no worker credit, a family of four would hit the ceiling at about {{ usd(reachFour.low) }} to {{ usd(reachFour.high) }} of income, depending on the model (later with a credit). One person, whose ceiling is {{ usd(costPerPerson) }}, would hit it at about {{ usd(reachOne.low) }} to {{ usd(reachOne.high) }}. If that is too low, the ceiling can be set at twice the cost of coverage instead of once; the principle is the same.

2. A soft cap

This is the version the board considered and left out. Above a threshold the rate drops instead of stopping, so the contribution still rises with income, only more slowly. It gives up less money than a hard ceiling and keeps more of the progressive shape, at the price of being harder to explain.

3. A lower rate on more income

Shrink the exempt amount and lower the rate. More households pay something, each pays a smaller share, and the top bill falls with the rate. The board weighed this and recommended the higher exemption in January after public comment, though two of the five models still start lower. It is the least targeted of the three: it lowers the top by raising the bottom.

What it costs, honestly

A ceiling gives up revenue, and I have not modeled how much. It is billions of dollars a year: part of the ${{ stake.low }} billion to ${{ stake.high }} billion the top 1% would pay, plus something from the single people and couples below them who would also reach it. Whatever the number is, it has to come from somewhere: a slightly higher rate below the ceiling, the payroll tax, or the plan's budget. The finance committee said the same thing about a soft cap, that it "requires explicit offsets elsewhere." The board has actuaries who can price that. I would like to see them do it before the Legislature picks a model.

There is a fair argument on the other side, and the board made it: any cap makes the structure less progressive. Put bluntly, a ceiling is a tax cut for the highest earners that everyone below it, or the plan's budget, pays for. The committee also said the avoidance it worried about "may be limited for most households." All of that is true. My answer is that a health plan is a service with a cost, and contributing {{ ceilingTimes }} times the average is already progressive. Contributing {{ topTimes }} times the average, as the family at $2,000,000 would under one model, is something else.
