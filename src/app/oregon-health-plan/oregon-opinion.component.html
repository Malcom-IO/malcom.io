Opinion · Marcus Malcom

Pick Model 3, with three changes

This is one person's opinion. The explainer takes no position on the plan, and nothing here changes it. The three changes and every figure marked my math are mine, not the board's.

The board drafted five ways to pay for the plan and recommended none. I would take the third, and fix what it gets wrong.

my math The board's Model 2 is Model 3 with a 50% credit: same exemption, same payroll tax, a 12.05% rate against 7.69%. Halfway between them is {{ pct1(rateRange.low) }}, and that is the low end: a credit cannot exceed the bill it comes off, and a smaller credit runs into that limit less often, so more of it gets used. If all of it were used the rate would be {{ pct1(rateRange.high) }}. So change 1 alone puts the rate near {{ pct1(rate) }}. It is a range from two of the board's own models, not an actuary's figure.

my math The slower climb starts at twice what a household buying its own coverage pays today in the board's examples: {{ usd(thresholds.one) }} for one person (twice $6,900) and {{ usd(thresholds.four) }} for a family of four (twice $22,871). Above that, a quarter of each further dollar is still paid. Pays more than under Model 3
  • Households with no employer behind their income, below the top: the self-employed, retirees with income, investors. Their rate goes from 7.7% to about {{ pct1(proposal) }}, and a self-employed family of four pays more than under Model 3 up to about {{ usd(topCrossover) }}.
  • Higher earners with an employer. A family of four with a private employer pays more than under Model 3 above about {{ usd(employedCrossover) }}: the credit no longer covers the higher rate.
  • Workers at employers under $500,000 of payroll. No payroll tax is paid on their wages, so there is no credit, and their rate still rises.
  • Progressivity, at the very top. The board left a soft cap out because it makes the structure less progressive. That is true of change 2, and it is the point of it.
Pays less than under Model 3
  • Most workers up to about the middle, public employees most of all. A family of four with a private employer pays less up to about {{ usd(employedCrossover) }}. Not all: the couple at $85,000 pays about $150 more.
  • Households at the top, above about {{ usd(topCrossover) }} for a self-employed family of four.
  • The plan itself, if the appendix is right that without a credit there is "a large financial incentive" to turn employees into contractors, and if fewer of the highest earners leave.

Four smaller fixes

{{ usd100(marriagePenalty) }}

End the marriage penalty

Two single people exempt $63,840 between them; a married couple, $43,280. That costs a couple this much a year. Set the couple's exemption at twice the single's. my math

{{ usd(splitSaving) }}

Count related firms as one

Each $500,000 exemption is worth this much a year, so splitting one firm into several pays. The draft has no rule against it. my math

14%

Treat public employers like private ones

School districts, cities and the state would pay the top payroll rate from the first dollar, because they spend more of payroll on coverage today. In the board's example that is still less than the premium they pay now, but it is one public budget taxing another.

55%

Write in a fallback

More than half the money is the payroll tax, which a federal benefits law could be used to challenge. The committee said it "may want automatic triggers" on other taxes if that happens. None is written.

I cannot price the first and third: the documents do not give the number of married returns above the exemption or the size of public payrolls.

What I looked at and set aside

A hard ceiling

A household at $1,000,000 and one at $2,000,000 would pay the same.

A lower rate on more income

Lowering the exemption to 138% of poverty only gets the rate to about 6.6% to 7.0%, and it charges households that pay nothing today. my math

Moving money from payroll to households

Households would see more of the cost, but payroll tax is paid before federal tax, and the report does not say a household contribution would be. It would likely send more of Oregon's money to Washington.

What I would ask for: have the board's actuaries price Model 3 with a worker credit at a few levels and a slower climb at a few starting points, at both cost estimates, and show who pays more and who pays less, before the Legislature picks a model. My figures for the slower climb are rough; theirs would not be.
